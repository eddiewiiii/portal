Monday morning, he sent me a photo from the high-speed train. 

Outside the window, the sky was grey, with clouds hanging low over the horizon. 

His only caption was: “Looks like it wants to rain.”

I stared at the photo for a few seconds. A line from a Karen Mok song came to mind: a cloudy day, in a room with the lights off.

"Maybe Changsha is in a bad mood today."

"Maybe it's upset because I left."

"Yeah. It doesn't know whether to cry."

"It doesn't want me to go."

"So the rain can't make up its mind."

I paused after sending that.

Somehow, this no longer felt like the kind of conversation friends usually have.

He came back to Changsha for a short vacation. Today, he had to leave.

We had talked easily these past few days. The kind of conversations where you don't have to look for a topic. You say whatever comes to mind, and the other person catches it and throws something back.

But it was only a few days.

Soon he would be back at work, and everything would return to a screen.

Changsha didn't rain that day.

I went out in the morning. The sky was already gray, heavy with clouds, and the air clung to my skin. I looked up at the entrance to my building. The clouds were thick enough to feel as if they might collapse at any moment.

But nothing happened.

All day, I kept glancing out the window.

Still grey.

Still nothing.

My phone buzzed throughout the day. Mostly him.

On the train.

Arrived.

Unpacking.

Getting off.

The conversation slowly drifted from what we had eaten to what I was doing that night, then somehow to what I was thinking about.

"Nothing," I said.

"Liar."

In the afternoon, the sky darkened another shade.

I sat at my desk, looking out at the pale gray light.

Then my phone buzzed again.

I read his message several times.

My fingers stayed on the screen for a moment.

I'm not going to write what he said.

But I knew.

This wasn't regular chatting anymore.

At eight, I got off work.

I pushed open the glass door, and a wave of wet air hit me.

It was raining.

Heavy rain struck the pavement. Under the streetlights, I could see it falling in thick, steady lines.

I stood beneath the awning and watched for a while.

Changsha had hesitated all day.

Then, finally, it rained.

My phone buzzed in my pocket.

I took it out.

It was him.

I replied with one word, put my phone away, opened my umbrella, and walked into the rain.
