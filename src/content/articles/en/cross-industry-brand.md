The first thing I noticed was the air conditioner. It was too cold. I was sitting across from a potential client in a meeting room in Tsim Sha Tsui. The slides were ready. The proposal was prepared. I had gone through every possible question in my head before walking in.

But after the first few minutes, I realized something. The room was quiet. Not the comfortable kind of quiet. The kind where you know the other person is listening, but not yet believing.

I continued explaining. The company. The service. The value we could create. I tried to answer every concern before they even asked. The more I talked, the more I felt the distance between us. It was not because the proposal was bad. It was because trust had not arrived yet.

After the meeting, I walked out into the streets of Tsim Sha Tsui. The city was noisy. People were rushing past. Cars were moving. But my mind was still in that cold meeting room. I kept thinking about one question. Why do some people trust you, while others don't?

Later, I realized that trust is rarely created by one perfect presentation. It is not something you can force into existence. A client does not trust you because you say the right words. They trust you because, over time, your actions match your words. You answer when you say you will. You solve problems when they appear. You keep promises when nobody is watching.

Small things. Repeated many times. That is how trust is built.

Looking back, I think I used to misunderstand sales. I thought sales was about convincing people. Finding the right arguments. Finding the right way to explain. But slowly, I learned that the best salespeople are not always the best speakers. They are often the people who understand others the most. They listen. They notice hesitation. They understand that sometimes a not yet is not rejection. It is simply a request for more time.

The air conditioner in that meeting room was still running when I left. Maybe the room was cold because of the temperature. Or maybe it was because two people were still strangers.

Years later, I still remember that room. Not because I won the deal. I don't even remember what happened afterward. I remember it because that was one of the first times I understood. Trust cannot be presented. It has to be experienced.
